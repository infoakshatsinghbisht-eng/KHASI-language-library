Introduction
The family Theraphosidae Thorell, 1869, currently comprising 
1,156 species in 186 genera worldwide (World Spider Catalog, 
2026), represents the most speciose lineage within the infraorder 
Mygalomorphae. In India, theraphosids are represented by six 
subfamilies, namely Eumenophorinae Pocock 1897, 
Ischnocolinae Simon 1892, Poecilotherinae Schmidt 1995, 
Selenocosmiinae Simon 1889, Selenogyrinae Hirst 1908, 
Thrigmopoeinae Pocock 1900, with 67 described species in 16 
genera. Among these, the subfamily Selenocosmiinae Simon, 
1892 is represented in India by four genera: Chilobrachys 
Karsch, 1892; Lyrognathus Pocock, 1895; Haplocosmia Schmidt 
& von Wirth, 1996; and Selenocosmia Ausserer, 1871, 
comprising a total of 16 species (World Spider Catalog, 2026).
The genus Selenocosmia currently comprises 41 valid species 
and is widely distributed across South Asia, Southeast Asia, and 
the Australasian region (World Spider Catalog). 
In India, the genus has historically been represented by two 
species: Selenocosmia sutherlandi Gravely, 1935 and 
Selenocosmia kulluensis Chamberlin, 1917, both recorded from 
the Himalayan biogeographic region and described exclusively 
on the basis of male specimens. However, several Indian 
species historically assigned to Selenocosmia have been re-
evaluated following detailed taxonomic revisions. Selenocosmia

During recent field surveys conducted through Meghalaya in 
Northeast India additional selenocosmiine material was 
collected. Detailed morphological examination revealed an 
undescribed species of Selenocosmia, diagnosable by the 
presence of unilobed spermathecae, a well-developed maxillary 
stridulatory lyra composed of scimitar-shaped lyra setae, non-
incrassate tibia IV, and the absence of dense penicillate 
retrolateral setal brushes on tibia and metatarsus IV (Yu et al., 
2021, Lin et al., 2022).
Here, we describe a new species of the genus Selenocosmia 
from India, Selenocosmia meghalayensis sp. nov., based on 
material collected from Meghalaya.

Abstract. – We describe a new species: Selenocosmia meghalayensis sp. nov. from East Khasi Hills, 
Meghalaya, north eastern India, based on three female specimens. The new species is diagnosed by a unique 
combination of morphological characters, with particular emphasis on the structure of the spermathecae, as 
well as somatic characters including coloration, setation, and morphometrics. Detailed descriptions and 
measurements, are provided. Notes on habitat and distribution map are included.

Methods
Specimens were hand-collected, and live individuals were 
photographed using a Canon 60D (Canon) equipped with a macro 
lens (60 mm) and Speedlight 430 EX II external flash. Left leg I of a 
holotype female (NRC-AA-3061) was collected in 99.9% molecular-
grade ethanol and stored at –20°C for genetic analyses. Specimens 
were then euthanized using isoflurane, fixed in 6% formaldehyde for 
~24 hours, washed and kept in tap water for ~12 hours, and then 
finally transferred to 70% ethanol for long-term storage. Spermathecae 
were dissected and cleared using needles under ZEISS Stemi 305 
stereo dissecting microscope, kept in clove oil for 1 hour, and 
transferred to 70% ethanol. The left chelicera of the holotype was 
dissected and stored in 70% ethanol. Measurements of body parts 
were obtained consistently from the left side of each specimen (except 
for damaged leg segments, which were obtained from the other side) 
using a Mitutoyo digital vernier calliper (to the nearest 0.01 mm). All 
measurements are in millimetres. The measurements of legs are 
shown as: total length (femur, patella, tibia, metatarsus, tarsus). Eye 
measurements: distances between eyes, measured at the narrowest 
points, and eyes diameter. The holotype female, along with two 
paratype females, are deposited in the Museum and Research 
Collection Facility at the National Centre for Biological Sciences 
(NCBS), Bengaluru, Karnataka, India. Comparative material for the 
study was examined from the collection of the Natural History 
Museum (NHMUK), London.

Etymology. – The specific epithet is a noun in apposition referring to 
Meghalaya, the Indian state from which the new species was collected.
Diagnosis. – This species closely resembles Selenocosmia 
longiembola in general habitus but It can be distinguished from 
Selenocosmia longiembola by the spermathecae, which are distally 
longitudinally flattened, directed outward, and form a semi-loop or 
U-shaped structure with a rounded apex. In contrast, Selenocosmia 
longiembola possesses elongate, unilobed spermathecae that are 
clearly defined and sclerotized and bear distinct stalks.

Spermatheca. – Unilobed long and slender, arising from a broad membranous

Distribution and natural history. – The burrows from which 
females were collected were situated on mud-bunds or mud 
walls at approximately ~1  m above ground and varied in size. 
Within a ~1   km² study area, a total of 20 burrows were 
recorded, indicating a moderate local density. Each burrow 
typically had a single silk-covered entrance, although double 
entrances were observed in three instances. The burrows were 
~400–500  mm deep, with an internal diameter of about 40  mm. 
Some individuals were observed at the burrow entrances (n=4), 
while in others, the entrances were completely covered with 
silk (n  =  5). (Fig 6). However, individuals from Garo hills, the 
burrows were close proximity around 1 ft with minimal silk 
surrounded the entrances. Interestingly one active burrow was 
recorded in a marshy area. The habitat in this region consisted 
of evergreen forest with dense canopy cover. Other theraphosid 
species, including Chilobrachys sp. and Lyrognathus sp., were 
also observed in close proximity. However, the observed 
individuals were juveniles and could not therefore be identified 
to the species-level.
At present, the species is known only from the Khasi Hills and 
Garo Hills, Meghalaya, India. Although the species currently 
occurs in relatively undisturbed habitats, increasing 
developmental activities such as mining, plantation expansion, 
and jhum cultivation in both the Khasi Hills and Garo Hills 
may pose potential threats to its population in the future.
Discussion
Selenocosmia was described by Ausserer in 1871 and presently 
contains over 41 known species globally, with widespread 
distribution across South Asia, Southeast Asia, and the 
Australasian region (World Spider Catalog, 2026). However, 
despite remarkable progress in systematic studies on tarantulas 
in recent times, the species diversity and taxonomy of 
Selenocosmia remain unrevised. The generic delimitation and 
relationships between Selenocosmia, Phlogius, Chilocosmia, 
and Selenopelma have long been disputed (Schmidt & von 
Wirth, 1992; Schmidt, 1995; Raven, 2000; Schmidt, 2015). 
Raven proposed to combine most genera and placed 
Selenopelma Schmidt & Krause, which was established based 
on the northern-Vietnamese species Selenopelma kovariki 
Schmidt & Krause, 1995, as a junior synonym of Selenocosmia 
(Raven, 1985; Raven, 2000). In a recent taxonomic study on 
Chinese Selenocosmiinae, Yu et al. (2022) examined the 
topotypes of Selenopelma kovariki and considered that most 
Selenocosmia species from China are closely related to 
Selenopelma kovariki and show some characters obviously 
different from the type species Selenocosmia javanensis). 
Nonetheless, most Chinese species were assigned to 
Selenocosmia based on Raven’s work (Zhu & Zhang, 2008). 
Similarly, based on morphology, Selenocosmia meghalayensis 
resembles northern-Vietnamese Selenopelma-like species and 
shows obvious distinction from Selenocosmia javanensis 
(unilobed vulva, longitudinally flat distal spermathecae, with 
rounded edges, needle-formed strikers, and a sharp process at 
the tip of stridulating setae in Selenocosmia meghalayensis 
versus bilobed and distally bulbous spermathecae, scimitar-
shaped stickers, and tip of stridulating setae without a sharp

subsequent records or modern redescriptions.
Overall, the taxonomy of Selenocosmia remains poorly resolved, with 
longstanding uncertainty regarding its delimitation and relationships 
with allied genera. The present study is also constrained by some 
limitations. First, the description of Selenocosmia meghalayensis sp. nov. 
is based solely on female specimens, while the two previously known 
Indian species (Selenocosmia sutherlandi and Selenocosmia 
kulluensis) are known only from male specimens, preventing direct 
comparison of both sexes across the Indian representatives of the 
genus. Second, the absence of molecular data limits our ability to test 
the phylogenetic placement of the new species and evaluate the 
broader generic boundaries within Selenocosmiinae. These gaps 
highlight the need for integrative taxonomic studies combining 
morphology and molecular phylogenetics, along with targeted field 
surveys to obtain both sexes and fresh material from across the 
distribution range of Indian and Southeast Asian Selenocosmia. Such 
efforts will be paramount to clarify species boundaries, resolve generic 
relationships, and achieve a stable taxonomic framework for this 
complex group of tarantulas.
