1 have gone through the poem entitled “Jingkmen Ba 
La Bam Hynroh” or “Joy under duress” composed by 
B. Chedrack Jyrwa. The peom centres round the life of 
u Rang who is the main prop of the family, and who has made 
useful contribution towards its solidarity and prosperity. 
As time goes on u Rang is allured by the temptations of 
this world, and soon becomes an easy prey to the desiies 
of the flesh and the vain longings of the heart. The 
family which is built up on the edifice of love soon crum-

bles, and the forces of disentegration hold the sway. 
There is no sign of a stiver lining. All is lost. In the 
midst of deepening crisis, u Skhem comes to the scene. By 
slow degrees he tiies to wean back u Rang from the world 
of pleasure into the blazing light of the supreme Being 
bringing new possibilities of a regenerated life. 

The peom ends in a happy note with the reunion of 
u Rang with his wife and family. 

The poet does not adhere to the poetic norms, does 
not make use of rhythm and metre or any metrical orna- 
ments. While use uf b]«ink verse or free verse has not the 
music of rhyme which enables a reader to pay attention 
to the pause and cadence, it is susceptible of greater di- 
versification of manner, and is well suited to long epic 
poems where rhymed verses must be monotonous. The 
present work is more or less similar to the structure of 
Hebrew poetry especially in the utterances of the pro- 

g hets of the 8th centuiy B. C. using among others the 
irm of parallelism. It may be mentioned that some of the 
world’s best poets made use of blank verse. Shakespeares’s 
dramas, Milton’s Paradise Lost, Tennyson’s Idylles of 
the kings are some of the more glaring examples. On the 
whole one senses the use of similes or metaphors designed 
to give a more vivid, more lively mode of illosttation and 
&ese when addressed to the heart serve also to please.

There is one more point to consider. How far the 
poet succeeds in preserving the purity of langauge

based on the chaste Khasi used by the {>eople of Cherra* 
punjec ? Are there offences against the purity of words ? 
In modern Khasi literature there is a growing tendency 
to make use of words taken from other sub-cultural back- 
grounds or ethos as this process is expected to enrich the 
language so as to include various dimension into one ambit. 
The purists may object to this on the ground that offences 
against the purity of words may have an unhealthy effect 
on the accepted norm so faithfully adhered to by the 
literary giants of the beginning and upto the middle of 
the century. 

On the whole there is loftiness of thought and splendid 
dignity of expression though we search in vain for the 
variety^ flexibility and lyric passion^ and this to me 
appears to be the main drawback of the poem. 

The poet seems to have been exposed to a variety of 
contemporai y influences. The Psalms, the Book of Job 
and perhaps the parable of the Prodigal Son of the New 
Testament must have exerted great influences in the mind 
of the poet. Influences may be traced also to the writings 
of Teilhard De Chardin, that controversial Roman Ca- 
tholic theologian dealing with the destiny of man and to 
Rabindranath Tagore’s Gitanjali which pre-supposes the 
existence of a greater Being beyond our life who shaped 
and guides our destiny. TheVe is obviouily nothing wrong 
in this. A poet absorbs into his sphere all the light which 
is anywhere radiating. The question is not where the 
poet draws his tap-roots for nourishment, but the rea) 
question, is what is the culminating expression of that 
nourishment — what blossom is forthcoming. The efficacy of 
a poet depends on how far he is able to trausmuta the 
baser, coarser material into, perhaps, pure 
and in this attempt the poet seems to nave admirably
