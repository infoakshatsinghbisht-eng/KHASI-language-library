Brahmaputra. The question of effecting direct railway 
communication with Burma has been reopened and 
various alignments have been suggested and are under 
consideration.

CHAPTER VIII. 

General Administration. 

The district was resettled in 1911-12. The method 
of assessment was the now well-known soil-unit system ment * 
introduced by Sir Bampfylde Fuller in 1902 in Kamrup 
and SibBagar. The application of the old and new rates 
to the area settled at attestation resulted in an increase of 
11*43 per cent, in the revenue of the district. In North 
Lakhimpur the enhancement, mainly due to surrey, was 
under 3 per cent, while in the Sadr subdivision after the 
exclusion of the two mauzas which are economically part 
of North Lakhimpur there was an enhancement of 14 8 
per cent. That the new assessment was moderate is 
shown by the fact that it represents only 14 per cent, of 
the value of the gross produce of the area cropped. 

Sudden increase in revenue was prevented by the applica- 
tion of the rules for progressive enhancement. 

Nisf-khirqj estates were assessed on cultivation, an.d 
their assessment Bhows an increase of 16*2 per cent. 

Lakhir;oj lands were classed and revenue calculated on 
the cultivated area for the purpose of calculating local 
rates. 

The period of settlement was for twenty years from 
April 1911 in North Lakhimpur subdivision and in the 
Dibrugarh assessment group, and from April 1912 in the 
rest of the Dibrugarh subdiyision.

About the middle of March 1911, Mr. Noel William- 
son, Assistant Political Officer at Sadiya, accompanied ^ 
by Br. J. 1). Gregorson and a considerable, number of hi* party, 
servants and coolies started on a tour in the Abor 
country. The party left Passighat on the 18th March 
and arrived at Botung on the 20th. A few dayB later 
it was split up into two, Mr. Williamson going on a 
little ahead, while Br. Gregorson stayed behind with 
some sick coolies. On the 80th March Br. Gregorson’s 
party was treacherously attaoked at Pangi by the Abors, 
and Br. Gregorson and practically all those with him 
were murdered. The same fate overtook Mr. William- 
son and his men on the following day at Komsing. Of 
all those present only five ooones escaped with their 
lives. 

The season was now too far advanced to despatch Abor op* 
immediately a punitive expedition into this little jjjjJJjj “ 
known and difficult country. Enquiries were however 
at once instituted to disoover what villages were respon- 
sible, and in the following October a strong force under 
General Bower started to avenge the massacre, and

finally to convince these turbulent tribesmen that the 
British Government could not thus be insulted with 
impunity. The expedition returned at the end of 
March 1912 after these objects had been fully accom- 
plished. Some of the actual murderers were captured, 
and were subsequently tried. Five in all were executed, 
and others were sentenced to long terms of imprison' 
ment. 

Formation of A further and not the least important result of the 
Nortk- Abor Expedition was the formation of the North-East 

^Di^‘ Frontier District, Formerly portion of this district 
was known as the Dibrugarh Frontier Tract, and was 
administered by an Assistant Political Officer with 
headquarters at Sadiya, subordinate to the Deputy 
Commissioner, Lakhimpur. After the expedition of 
1911-12, this system of administration was changed. 
The Dibrugarh Frontier Tract ceased to exist, and the 
district of Sadiya now considerably enlarged became 
a separate entity controlled by a Political Officer, work- 
ing directly under the Chief Commissioner, with three 
Assistant Political Officers; of these one is posted at 
Passighat, and is broadly speaking iu charge of the 
Abor Hills. A detailed description of the boundary 
of the newly formed district will be found in the 
Government of India Gazette Notification No. 977E.-B., 
dated the 25th September 1914. Further modifications 
of the district boundary will be found described in 
Notifications Nos. 978 and 979E-B. of the same date.
